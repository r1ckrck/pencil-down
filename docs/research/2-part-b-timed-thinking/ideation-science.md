# Ideation Science for Timed Design Thinking (CEED Part B prep)

Scope: cognitive science and design-education evidence on (a) generating design ideas fast, (b) whether that is trainable, (c) choosing and committing under time. Most sources were reached as abstracts or secondary summaries, not full text; this is flagged per item. Terms: **divergent thinking** = producing many varied answers to an open prompt; **fluency** = count of ideas; **originality** = statistical rarity / judged novelty; **fixation** = getting stuck reproducing an example or first idea; **incubation** = a break from the problem before returning.

## 1. Is ideation fluency / divergent thinking trainable?

### Takeaway
Yes, with moderate-to-large effects that replicate across three independent meta-analyses (d ≈ 0.5–0.75). Gains are largest on divergent-thinking and problem-solving measures, smaller on real-world performance, and figural (drawing-based) training material tends to work better, which suits a sketch-based exam.

### Cited Findings
- **Scott, Leritz & Mumford (2004)**, *Creativity Research Journal* 16(4), 361–388: meta-analysis of 70 studies / 97 effect sizes. Mean effects by outcome: divergent thinking ≈ .75, problem solving ≈ .84, performance ≈ .35, attitudes/behaviour ≈ .24 — [UVic thesis summarising Scott et al.](https://dspace.library.uvic.ca/bitstreams/b37e9fbc-05d8-4b41-b192-4969288bacc1/download); full text likely at [CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/357532567.pdf) (not verified by me)
- Scott et al. overall effect reported as d = 0.68 (0.64 after outlier correction) — [Increasing Intelligence blog summary](https://increasingintelligence.blogspot.com/2017/02/creativity-training.html); d = .68 also cited in [Creativity: Theories–Research–Applications 2024 review](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/ctra-2024-0007.pdf). Secondary sources only.
- Scott et al. covered only studies published before 2001 — [Haase et al. accepted MS, via Essex repository listing](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/34344/)
- **Haase, Hanel & Gronau (2023)**, *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*: 332 effect sizes, 12 method clusters. Overall Hedges' g = 0.53, 95% CI [0.44, 0.61] (≈70% of trained participants beat the average control). Complex training courses, meditation and cultural exposure most effective (g ≈ 0.66); drugs least (g = 0.10, n.s.). Figural methods more effective than others; convergent-thinking enhancement more effective than divergent-thinking enhancement. High heterogeneity; no publication-bias evidence found — [University of Bath research portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/creativity-enhancement-methods-for-adults-a-meta-analysis/) (abstract-level; subgroup CIs not verified)
- **Ma (2009)**, *Creativity Research Journal* 21, 30–42: ~111 studies, 2,013 effect sizes; unweighted grand mean 0.69, sample-weighted 0.72. Problem-solving and verbal creativity measures showed larger effects than nonverbal/emotional ones. "Defining the problem" (problem finding) among variables with large mean effects — [NCCU repository record](https://ah.lib.nccu.edu.tw/handle/140.119/78241); claim that problem finding was the *greatest* predictor comes from a secondary paper — [PMC8339255](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8339255)
- Ma (2006) earlier meta-analysis: grand mean 0.77 across 34 studies — [Increasing Intelligence blog](https://increasingintelligence.blogspot.com/2017/02/creativity-training.html) (secondary)

### Inferences
- Trainability is not in doubt; the open question is transfer. Effects shrink from ~.75 (lab DT tests) to ~.35 (performance), so the app's drills should mirror the real exam task types rather than generic "alternate uses" tasks.
- Haase's finding that figural material trains better and that convergent (selecting/refining) skills train more readily than divergent ones supports building explicit *selection* drills, not just generation drills — directly relevant to her stated "decide and move on" weakness.
- "Complex training courses" outperforming single tricks implies a curriculum (multiple techniques + practice + feedback) beats one technique.

### Gaps
- Could not access full texts to report per-technique effects inside Scott et al. (they reported cognitive, idea-generation-focused training doing best — from memory, not verified here; treat as unconfirmed).
- No meta-analytic data found specifically on *speed* of ideation as a trainable outcome separate from fluency counts.

## 2. Design fixation: causes and remedies

### Takeaway
Fixation is robust: seeing an example (or one's own first idea) pulls later ideas toward it. Defixation instructions, analogies from distant domains, incubation, and pushing for more ideas reduce it, but effects are inconsistent across studies and best treated as "helpful, not guaranteed."

### Cited Findings
- **Jansson & Smith (1991)**, *Design Studies* 12(1), 3–11: origin of the definition — "blind adherence to a set of ideas or concepts limiting the output of conceptual design"; first experimental paradigm (show an example, measure copying of its features) — cited in [Linsey et al., JMD preprint](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/Schunn/papers/JMD041003.pdf) and [AI EDAM, Linsey et al. on design-by-analogy and fixation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ai-edam/article/abs/overcoming-design-fixation-design-by-analogy-studies-and-nonintuitive-findings/038760839BBBEFC08F146457A77BBE51)
- **Defixation instructions**: using Jansson & Smith's fixating examples, Chrysikou & Weisberg found that adding explicit instructions to avoid the example's flaws can negate its fixating effect — [Linsey et al., JMD preprint](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/Schunn/papers/JMD041003.pdf) (single-study basis; later replications mixed per reviews)
- Linsey et al. compared control vs. example-shown vs. example + defixation materials and reported fixation "can be mitigated" — [AI EDAM](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ai-edam/article/abs/overcoming-design-fixation-design-by-analogy-studies-and-nonintuitive-findings/038760839BBBEFC08F146457A77BBE51)
- Key reviews (no pooled meta-analysis located): Vasconcelos & Crilly (2016) "Inspiration and fixation: Questions, methods, findings, and challenges," *Design Studies* 42, 1–32; Crilly & Cardoso (2017) "Where next for research on fixation…"; Alipour et al. (2018) review of fixation factors; Youmans (2011) on physical prototyping and group work reducing fixation — cited in [Wadinambiarachchi et al., arXiv 2403.11164](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.11164)
- A design-education study reported a negative correlation between number of ideas produced and fixation ratio (more ideas ↔ less fixation) — [Design Society, "Overcoming design fixation through education and creativity methods"](https://www.designsociety.org/publication/34578/overcoming_design_fixation_through_education_and_creativity_methods) (correlational, single study; attribution from search snippet, verify)
- **Incubation** (Sio & Ormerod 2009, *Psychological Bulletin* 135, 94–120): positive overall incubation effect; divergent-thinking tasks benefit more than insight tasks; longer preparation before the break → bigger effect; filling the break with demanding tasks → smaller effect; low-demand activity can beat pure rest — [Lancaster eprint](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/56211) (abstract-level; pooled d not retrieved)

### Inferences
- In CEED, the main fixation source is self-generated: the first idea for "a product from a cylinder" anchors the rest. App mechanics: force N quick thumbnails before any commitment; prompt "now one that shares nothing with idea 1."
- Within a 24-min question, a true incubation break is impossible, but a mini-version is: interleave — rough-generate for Q1, move to Q2, return. Sio & Ormerod suggest the break works better after solid preparation and with a low-demand filler, which maps to "generate hard, then do mechanical rendering on another question."
- Avoid showing her worked example answers *before* she attempts a prompt; show them after (examples prime fixation).

### Gaps
- No quantitative meta-analysis of defixation interventions found; effect sizes unavailable.
- Did not find studies on fixation specifically in timed drawing exams.

## 3. Time pressure and creativity

### Takeaway
Pressure is not uniformly harmful: what hurts is high *evaluative* threat and loss of control; mild evaluation and focused, uninterrupted pressure can help. People feel more creative under pressure than they are. Short, known, controllable time boxes are the safer design.

### Cited Findings
- **Byron, Khazanchi & Nazarian (2010)**, *J. Applied Psychology* 95(1), 201–212: 76 experiments / 82 samples. Low-evaluative contexts *increased* creative performance vs. control; highly evaluative contexts decreased it (curvilinear). Uncontrollability had a linear negative effect. Effect depends on stressor type and intensity — [abstract via SUFE academic newsletter](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/371445) (effect sizes not retrieved)
- Later meta-analysis (Huang & Yu): overall stress → creativity d = −0.20; adults most hurt by time pressure — reported in search summaries citing [Frontiers in Psychology 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1049174/full) (attribution of which paper said what is uncertain; verify)
- Song et al. (2022) meta-analysis: J-shaped time pressure → innovation performance relation — same [Frontiers 2022 article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.1049174/full); conflicts with inverted-U framing
- Zhang et al.: inverted-U between time pressure and *incremental* creativity; time pressure hampered *radical* creativity — [PolyU repository](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/108933/1/Zhang_Time_Pressure_Creativity.pdf) (single field study)
- **Amabile, Hadley & Kramer (2002)**, HBR "Creativity Under the Gun": 177 employees, 7 companies, 9,000+ diary entries. Creativity generally lower under high time pressure, with a lag ("pressure hangover") into the next two days; people *reported feeling* more creative under pressure. Exception: pressure helped when people could focus undisturbed on one important task ("on a mission"); distractions hurt more than the clock — [APA Monitor, Nov 2002](https://www.apa.org/monitor/nov02/tickingclock)
- "45% less likely to think creatively on highest-pressure days" — [Zapier blog](https://zapier.com/blog/jumpstart-creativity/) (secondary; verify against original)

### Inferences
- The exam's pressure is focused and controllable (one task, known clock) — the "on a mission" condition — so the danger is less the clock than *evaluative fear* and indecision eating the clock.
- App design: make practice timers visible and predictable (controllability); keep judgement low-stakes during generation (Byron's low-evaluative benefit); separate a generation phase from an evaluation phase.
- Subjective "felt creative" is unreliable (Amabile) — log objective markers (idea count, time to commit) rather than self-rating.
- Exam tasks mostly reward incremental/competent originality, which Zhang suggests tolerates moderate pressure better than radical originality.

### Gaps
- Found no controlled study of design ideation specifically with ~20–25 min limits vs. longer limits.
- Byron et al.'s verdict on time pressure *specifically* (vs. evaluation) not retrieved.

## 4. Structured ideation techniques: which help novices fast?

### Takeaway
Best direct evidence for novices in design is Design Heuristics (small classroom studies, same research group, consistently positive). SCAMPER, Crazy 8s, brainwriting, morphological analysis and forced connections have weak or no peer-reviewed effect-size evidence in my search; they are plausible prompt generators, not proven ones.

### Cited Findings
- **Design Heuristics** (Yilmaz, Daly, Seifert et al.; 77 cards derived from expert designers' work). Daly et al. (2012), *Int. J. Engineering Education* 28(2), 463–473: 48 first-year engineering students, 12 cards. Concepts with cards were rated more developed and creative; students applied one heuristic several ways; concepts without cards were "often replications of known ideas or minor changes to existing products" — [IJEE PDF](https://www.ijee.ie/articles/Vol28-2/29_ijee2559ns.pdf)
- Kramer et al. (2015), *Advances in Engineering Education*: sophomore *industrial design* students using 12 cards produced more diverse and creative concepts; uptake uneven — some students struggled to apply heuristics — [AEE PDF](https://advances.asee.org/wp-content/uploads/vol04/issue04/Papers/AEE-16-Kramer.pdf)
- Heuristics support two modes: initiating ideas and transitioning between concepts — [ASEE paper](https://strategy.asee.org/design-heuristics-support-two-modes-of-idea-generation-initiating-ideas-and-transitioning-among-concepts.pdf)
- Cross-disciplinary case study: novice teams generated innovative solutions with cards, but only 3 of 8 teams showed abstraction across phases — [Iowa State repository](https://dr.lib.iastate.edu/handle/20.500.12876/44680)
- **Analogy**: design-by-analogy can reduce fixation, with "nonintuitive findings" (distance and presentation of analogies matter) — [Linsey et al., AI EDAM](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ai-edam/article/abs/overcoming-design-fixation-design-by-analogy-studies-and-nonintuitive-findings/038760839BBBEFC08F146457A77BBE51)
- Haase et al. (2023): figural methods and complex multi-component training most effective — [Bath portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/creativity-enhancement-methods-for-adults-a-meta-analysis/)

### Inferences
- Design Heuristics are the best fit for the "product from a primitive shape" question (heuristics like "nest," "fold," "hollow out," "add natural features" map directly onto transforming a cylinder/cube). Use a small rotating subset (Daly and Kramer both used 12, not 77) to keep choice overhead low.
- Uneven uptake means each card needs a worked visual example, and the app should track which cards she actually uses productively.
- Crazy 8s (8 sketches in 8 min) is untested in literature but is structurally a quantity goal + time box, matching the fixation/quantity findings — reasonable as a warm-up format, label as practitioner method.
- Different exam questions likely need different prompt sets: heuristics (product form), viewpoint/camera prompts (unusual POV scene), metaphor/visual-pun prompts (symbol story, icons), observation checklists (problem identification).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed effect sizes found for SCAMPER, Crazy 8s, brainwriting (individual), morphological analysis, or forced connections in novice design ideation. Not searched exhaustively (tool budget); brainwriting has group-level literature vs. verbal brainstorming that was not retrieved.
- Kudrowitz's quantity–quality ideation work not found.
- No head-to-head comparison of techniques under short time limits.

## 5. Deciding under time: selection, satisficing, persistence

### Takeaway
People are poor at selecting their own best idea, systematically favouring feasible/conventional over original, and they quit generating too early because they wrongly expect ideas to dry up. Explicit selection criteria shift choices toward originality, at some cost to felt satisfaction.

### Cited Findings
- **Rietzschel, Nijstad & Stroebe (2010)**, *British J. Psychology*: after generating ideas, participants chose poorly, mainly because they favoured feasible and desirable ideas over original ones. Exclusion instructions and quality-rating steps did not help. Explicitly instructing "pick the most creative/original" raised originality of picks but lowered satisfaction and rated effectiveness (effectiveness–originality trade-off) — [abstract via Zhenzhi](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/912990346)
- Later Rietzschel studies: little/no relationship between creativity of ideas generated and creativity of ideas selected — reported in [Watts dissertation, U. Oklahoma 2016](https://shareok.org/bitstream/11244/47052/5/2016_Watts_Logan_Dissertation.pdf) (secondary)
- **Berg (2016)**, *Administrative Science Quarterly* 61(3), 433–468: field study of 339 circus professionals + lab experiment. Creators forecast success of *others'* novel ideas better than managers, but not their own ideas; divergent thinking drives the advantage; managers undervalue novelty — [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/balancing-creative-high-wire-forecasting-success-novel-ideas)
- **Lucas & Nordgren (2020)**, "The Creative Cliff Illusion," *PNAS* 117(33), 19830–19836: 8 studies. Creativity stayed constant or rose across an ideation session, but people predicted decline; stronger belief in decline → fewer creative ideas. Illusion weaker in people with more creative experience and after an intervention explaining the effect — [Kellogg](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2020/the-creative-cliff-illusion/?p=1)

### Inferences
- Two opposing failure modes to design for: (a) quitting generation too early (creative cliff) and (b) never committing (her stated problem). A fixed generation window ("generate until minute 6, then must pick") handles both: it protects persistence and forces a decision.
- Since self-selection is weak and biased toward safe ideas, give her 2–3 explicit criteria tied to what CEED evaluators reward (e.g. "Is it unexpected? Can I draw it clearly in 15 min? Does it answer the brief?") — Rietzschel shows explicit originality criteria shift picks. Use satisficing (first idea that passes all criteria wins) rather than maximising.
- Berg suggests she judges others' ideas better than her own; an app mechanic could have her rate past ideas cold (a day later) to calibrate her own judgement.
- Teach the creative-cliff finding directly — Lucas & Nordgren's knowledge intervention reduced the illusion.

### Gaps
- No studies found on selection accuracy specifically under seconds-to-minutes time pressure.
- Satisficing vs. maximising literature (Simon; Schwartz) not searched within budget.

## 6. Problem finding / problem identification

### Takeaway
Problem finding is a distinct, consequential creative skill (linked to originality and career outcomes in art; modest-to-large associations in meta-analyses), but direct evidence that it is *trainable* via observation drills was not found in this search.

### Cited Findings
- **Getzels & Csikszentmihalyi** (1960s study, *The Creative Vision* 1976): art students at the School of the Art Institute of Chicago drew a still life. Students who spent longer exploring and reformulating the problem (handling objects, rearranging, restarting) produced paintings rated more original by independent experts and were more successful years later; intelligence did not predict studio performance or success — [MIT Press Reader](https://thereader.mitpress.mit.edu/the-1960s-art-school-experiment-that-redefined-creativity/)
- Follow-up timing reported inconsistently across secondary sources (5, 6, 7 and 18 years); dropout figures also vary (e.g. 8 of 11 low problem-finders left art vs. "about half") — same MIT Press source and [edcircuit](https://edcircuit.com/important-overlooked-aspect-of-creativity/) — treat specific numbers as unverified
- Critique: line between problem finding and problem solving is blurry; "new" problems are usually variants of conventional themes — [Project MUSE review](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/599015/summary)
- Meta-analysis (UGA dissertation): problem finding ↔ creativity r = .22 (95% CI .11–.32) across 40 studies — [UGA OpenScholar](https://openscholar.uga.edu/record/21269?ln=en)
- Ma (2009): problem definition among variables with large effects on creativity — [NCCU](https://ah.lib.nccu.edu.tw/handle/140.119/78241)

### Inferences
- Tension with her goal: Getzels' best students spent *more* time in problem formulation, which looks like the opposite of "decide fast." Resolution: budget the formulation explicitly (e.g. first 4–5 min of the problem-identification question for listing observed problems, then commit), rather than skipping it.
- For the "problem in an everyday setting" question, a structured observation scaffold (users, steps of an activity, failure points, workarounds, edge users) is a reasonable app feature, but its efficacy is inference, not evidence.

### Gaps
- No controlled studies found on training observation / problem identification in design students.
- Original 1976 book not consulted; follow-up details unverified.

## 7. Deliberate practice, timed repetition, reflection

### Takeaway
Within this search, evidence for practice comes indirectly from training meta-analyses (multi-component courses with practice beat single tricks). Direct evidence on timed repetition and post-attempt reflection for design ideation was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Complex training courses among the most effective enhancement methods (g ≈ 0.66) — [Haase et al. 2023, Bath portal](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/creativity-enhancement-methods-for-adults-a-meta-analysis/)
- Creative-cliff illusion weaker among people with more everyday creative experience — [Kellogg, Lucas & Nordgren 2020](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2020/the-creative-cliff-illusion/?p=1)
- Longer preparation before a break increases incubation benefits (practice depth matters) — [Sio & Ormerod 2009, Lancaster](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/56211)

### Inferences
- Structure each drill as: brief → timed generate (quantity goal) → forced pick with criteria → short sketch → 1-minute structured reflection (what anchored me, when did I commit, what would I pick now). Reflection's benefit is plausible from deliberate-practice theory but unverified here.
- Track per-question-type metrics (ideas generated, time-to-commit, technique used) across sessions so she sees improvement objectively — counters Amabile's felt-vs-actual mismatch.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: Ericsson-style deliberate-practice evidence applied to creative/design tasks; studies of reflection/debrief effects on ideation; spacing/interleaving effects on creative skill. These need a separate search.
